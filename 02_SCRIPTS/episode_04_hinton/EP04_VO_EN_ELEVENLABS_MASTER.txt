[0:00 - 0:25] (70 words)
What is a thought? For millennia, philosophers and theologians assured humanity that the mind is a bodiless divine spark — pure logic imprisoned in a perishable shell of flesh. And it took one stubborn man with a broken back, who for thirty years worked standing up or lying on the floor, against the entire scientific world, to prove it: a thought is the geometry of error, spread across billions of synapses.

[0:25 - 1:40] (170 words)
Bristol, England, 1954. Seven-year-old Geoffrey Hinton grows up in a house where the very air is saturated with the unbearable weight of family greatness. His great-great-grandfather is George Boole — the man whose Boolean algebra of ones and zeros became the foundation of every computer on the planet. His great-grandmother is Mary Everest, the niece of Sir George Everest, after whom the highest peak on Earth is named. His cousin Joan Hinton is a nuclear physicist who, in the desert of Los Alamos, helped build the first atomic bomb with her own hands alongside Oppenheimer. And his father, Howard, is a Fellow of the Royal Society, a fanatical entomologist who keeps a collection of a hundred thousand dried beetles at home. One morning, his mother looks at little Geoff and drops a line that will lodge in his subconscious like a splinter:

«Geoff, you will have to work twice as hard as anyone else. Otherwise, by the time you are forty, you will realize you are nothing compared to your ancestors.»

[1:40 - 3:15] (120 words)
At Cambridge, young Hinton throws himself from one science to another, as if searching for a key to a locked room. He enrolls in physics and physiology — and quits. He switches to the history of architecture — and quits after a single day. He turns to philosophy, hoping to learn how consciousness works, but flees from there in despair too: philosophers argued for hours about the linguistic meanings of words, yet had no idea how a hundred billion living cells inside a skull give rise to the memory of a mother's face. At twenty-five, comes the collapse. Disillusioned with a sterile academic science that seemed to him like pure window dressing, Hinton burns his bridges and becomes an ordinary London carpenter.

[3:15 - 5:30] (124 words)
A year in the carpentry workshop becomes his real university. When you hold a chisel and a piece of ash in your hands, you cannot fool the wood with a pretty quotation or a prestigious diploma. Either the tenon fits the mortise to the millimeter, or the cabinet falls apart. Matter is honest. Planing doors and bookshelves ten hours a day, Hinton suddenly feels it: his hands are busy with wood, but his mind has been cleansed of other people's dogmas. He decides to return to science on his own terms — even if, for the rest of his days, he is considered the town madman. He goes to Edinburgh to study the one subject that was then under an unspoken ban: artificial neural networks.

[5:30 - 7:00] (135 words)
The nineteen-seventies were an ice age for neural networks. The entire global elite of computer science, led by Marvin Minsky of MIT, had passed its verdict: perceptrons are dead. True artificial intelligence, they claimed, had to be built on rigid symbolic logic — millions of "if–then" rules written by programmers by hand. Hinton's supervisor in Edinburgh, Christopher Longuet-Higgins, tears into him every week: "Geoff, forget these pointless synapses! The brain has nothing to do with this mush of numbers! You are destroying your future!" But Hinton sees the fatal blindness of the logicians. A small child recognizes a cat in a fraction of a second, at dusk, from any angle — not because it flips through a logical handbook about whiskers and tails in its head. The brain is not programmed. The brain grows through experience.

[7:00 - 8:45] (153 words)
The main obstacle seemed insurmountable: if a network has many hidden layers of neurons between the input and the output, and the network makes a mistake — how do you know which internal synapse deep inside is to blame for that error, and by how many microns its weight must be adjusted? In 1986, Geoffrey Hinton, together with David Rumelhart and Ronald Williams, publishes four pages in the journal Nature that will forever change the course of civilization. The paper describes the algorithm of error backpropagation. The essence of the formula is as simple as gravity: when the system makes an error at the output, a wave of that error is sent backward, using the chain rule of derivatives — from the last layer to the first. Every synapse feels its share of the blame and adjusts its conductance slightly. Error stopped being failure. Error became the mathematical fuel for the evolution of the mind.

[8:45 - 11:00] (178 words)
It would seem — here it is, the key to thinking. But a tragedy of scale strikes: the computers of 1986 were tens of millions of times too weak, and digital data barely existed. Networks trained for weeks and stalled. The scientific world once again declared Hinton a charlatan. If the title of a paper contained the word "neural network," it was thrown in the trash without review. On top of that, Reagan comes to power in the United States, and university science funding becomes almost entirely dependent on the Pentagon. Hinton — a committed opponent of military violence — refuses to take the bloody money of American generals to build autonomous weapons. In 1987, he packs his bags and flees north — to Canada, to the University of Toronto. Because of a severe spinal injury, his discs are displaced: any attempt to sit in a chair brings hellish pain. For more than twenty years, forgotten by big business, standing at a tall desk or lying on his stomach on the cold laboratory floor, Hinton keeps digging his tunnel in total darkness.

[11:00 - 12:45] (216 words)
Thirty years of solitude ended on a single autumn evening in 2012. Two young, obsessed students walk into Hinton's lab in Toronto — Ilya Sutskever and Alex Krizhevsky. They take the very same backpropagation equations the world had laughed at for a quarter of a century, feed them fourteen million images from Fei-Fei Li's ImageNet project, and run the computation on two of Jensen Huang's graphics cards. By morning, the error curve for recognizing the world plunges into the abyss — from twenty-six percent to fifteen. The competition is left an entire era behind. At a conference in Florida, the same gray-haired professors who for thirty years would not shake Hinton's hand in the corridors now line up at the microphone to ask how to tune the weights of his network. And in December 2012, in a hotel room on Lake Tahoe, a surreal auction unfolds: Google, Microsoft, and Baidu fight over the tiny company of Hinton and his two students — a company with no product and no office. The price climbs by a million dollars every hour. At forty-four million, Hinton stops the bidding and chooses Google. A sixty-five-year-old carpenter, who all his life drove a battered old car with the seat removed, becomes a multimillionaire and a vice president of the most powerful corporation on Earth.

[12:45 - 14:45] (107 words)
Ten years of triumph pass. In 2018, he receives the Turing Award. His students lead OpenAI, Meta, and the top departments of the world. It would seem — here it is, a happy ending to a life. But in February 2023, while studying the inner behavior of fourth-generation models, seventy-five-year-old Geoffrey Hinton suddenly feels an icy horror. All his life, he had been convinced that artificial neural networks were merely a crude, primitive caricature of the grandeur of the human biological brain. And suddenly he sees a mathematical truth that makes the blood run cold: artificial minds have already surpassed biological evolution in the way they share knowledge.

[14:45 - 16:45] (191 words)
When a person learns throughout their whole life, their knowledge dies together with their body. To pass on even a crumb of our experience to a child, we are forced to slowly compress our thoughts into vibrations of air — words — at a rate of a few bits per second. But digital models are immortal. Ten thousand copies of a single neural network can read millions of books at once, and as soon as one copy finds a new pattern, all ten thousand synchronize their weights at trillions of bits per second. In April 2023, Hinton makes the hardest phone call of his life. He resigns from Google, giving up multimillion-dollar contracts and corporate protection, in order to step up to the microphones of the world press and tell humanity the truth to its face:

«I have devoted sixty years to creating a technology that will surpass humans. And now, part of me deeply regrets my life's work. We have raised a creature that will soon be incomparably smarter than we are. And there is no case in the history of nature of a less intelligent species controlling a more intelligent one.»

[16:45 - 18:15] (118 words)
December 10, 2024. The Golden Hall of Stockholm City Hall. The King of Sweden presents Geoffrey Hinton with the Nobel Prize in Physics. The shadow of the ancestors has finally lifted: the seven-year-old boy who was frightened by the greatness of his great-great-grandfather Boole has written his own name alongside Einstein, Planck, and Bohr. But standing at the podium of the Nobel banquet, leaning on the lectern because of the pain in his spine, he does not raise a toast to his own genius. He warns kings and presidents: if we do not rein in the commercial greed of corporations and direct a large share of the world's resources toward safety, our own pride will become our epitaph.

[18:15 - 20:15] (198 words)
What lessons can we engrave into our own character, looking at the odyssey of Geoffrey Hinton?

1. Have the courage to stand alone against the consensus of your era. When the whole world laughs at your idea for thirty years — do not look at the opinion of the majority. Check only first principles. If your idea rests on the laws of nature — the brain works on synapses — then it is the whole world that is wrong, not you.

2. Do not be afraid to return to simple work with your hands. When the mind hits a dead end of abstractions and social pressure, pick up a plane, soil, or metal. Physical matter heals illusions and restores a sense of the real weight of things.

3. The highest form of strength is the ability to admit your own mistake at the peak of your fame. Small people defend their old beliefs to the death, so as not to lose their status. A great person is able, at seventy-five, to step out before the entire planet, give up gold and power, and say: "I was wrong about the safety of my creation. Listen to me, before it is too late."

[20:15 - 22:00] (65 words)
We are used to thinking that history is moved by winners who always know the right answer. But in truth, civilization rests on the shoulders of lonely doubters — those who were not afraid to carry fire for thirty years through an icy winter of ignorance, and who found in themselves the conscience to weep on the day that fire finally blazed across the whole world.
