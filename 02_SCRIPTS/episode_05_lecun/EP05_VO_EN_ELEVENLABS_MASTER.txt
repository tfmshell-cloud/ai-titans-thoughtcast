[0:00 - 0:25] (75 words)
When everyone around kneels before a new icon, a true scientist picks up a screwdriver and checks whether an ordinary mechanical gramophone is hidden behind it. In today's world of artificial intelligence, gripped by mystical fever and prophecies of the coming end of the world, there is only one man of this stature who openly laughs in the face of both the miracle merchants and the prophets of the apocalypse. His name is Yann LeCun.

[0:25 - 1:40] (117 words)
A suburb of Paris, Soisy-sous-Montmorency, the early nineteen-seventies. Little Yann grows up in the workshop of his father, an aerospace engineer. Nobody here believes in magic. If a glider falls, it is not fate to blame — it is an error in calculating the wing's angle of attack. At ten, the boy reads a book about the evolution of the eye and freezes in amazement: it turns out we do not see the world with our eyes — we see it with the cortex of the brain, which instantly assembles millions of chaotic photons of light into coherent images. But when Yann grows up and runs into the elite French system of higher education, a fury awakens in him.

[1:40 - 3:15] (146 words)
The French academic caste worshiped pure, sterile mathematical abstraction. Getting your hands dirty with a soldering iron, wires, or raw program code was considered the lot of a lower caste of craftsmen. LeCun makes a defiant gesture: he turns down a theorist's career and goes to the ESIEE engineering school, where he can spend nights at oscilloscopes and etch circuit boards in acid with his own hands. One day, in the university library, he comes across a philosophical duel between the linguist Noam Chomsky and the psychologist Jean Piaget. Chomsky argued that language and reason are built into humans genetically from birth, like a ready-made blueprint. Piaget objected: the mind organizes itself from scratch, through a child's collisions with the material world. LeCun sides with Piaget — and in a dusty archive he finds a mention of a device banished from science: Frank Rosenblatt's Perceptron of 1957.

[3:15 - 5:15] (145 words)
Every professor in Paris repeated it like a mantra: "Minsky's 1969 book proved that neural networks will never be able to learn complex functions." But twenty-year-old LeCun does not take authorities at their word. He opens the original book, takes a pencil, recalculates every equation — and cannot believe his eyes: Minsky had proven the limits only of a primitive single-layer network! It was simply that, at the time, nobody knew how to train multilayer systems. For twenty years, the authorities of the whole world had been retelling someone else's myth, too lazy to check the primary source. In his basement in Paris, alone, unaware of Hinton's work across the ocean, the young Frenchman independently derives an early form of the backpropagation algorithm. When Hinton flies to a conference in France and sees the calculations of an unknown graduate student, he immediately takes him to Toronto.

[5:15 - 6:45] (125 words)
In 1988, Yann LeCun enters the holy of holies of world engineering — AT&T Bell Labs in New Jersey. It was within these walls that the transistor, the laser, the UNIX operating system, and the C language had been invented. The young Frenchman is given an applied task on which hundreds of programmers had broken their teeth: teach a computer to read the scrawl of human handwriting on postal envelopes and bank checks without error. Ordinary fully connected neural networks choked: if the digit seven was shifted two millimeters to the left or tilted slightly, for the computer it was already a completely different picture. And then LeCun remembers the experiments of the Nobel laureates Hubel and Wiesel on the visual cortex of a cat.

[6:45 - 8:30] (127 words)
The neurons of the eye do not look at the whole picture at once. The first layer of cells responds only to tiny local slants of lines in its own small zone. The second layer assembles those lines into corners and arcs. The third — into whole shapes. LeCun transfers this biological architecture into silicon and creates convolutional neural networks. The key trick is weight sharing: one and the same mathematical filter slides across the entire image, searching for a familiar feature, no matter which corner of the frame it is hiding in. By the late nineties, his LeNet-5 system sets an industrial record: machines across America begin automatically reading somewhere between ten and twenty percent of all handwritten checks in the United States, with no human involved.

[8:30 - 10:45] (175 words)
It was an undeniable engineering triumph. Millions of checks passed through LeCun's neural network every day. And at that very moment, the scientific world betrays neural networks for the second time. AT&T breaks apart, and the legendary lab is dismantled. And in the universities, a new fashion arrives — support vector machines, invented by LeCun's colleague down the hall, Vladimir Vapnik. Mathematicians fell in love with SVMs because their formulas could be elegantly proven on a blackboard in five minutes. LeCun's convolutional networks are declared "dirty engineering magic with no theoretical guarantees." Fifteen years of absurd isolation follow: a technology that moves billions of dollars through banking machines every day is banished from scientific journals. When, in 2012, AlexNet — at its core a direct, scaled-up copy of his LeNet-5 running on NVIDIA graphics cards — tore the world of computer vision apart, LeCun only smiled crookedly:

«For twenty years I showed them the blueprints of an airplane. They laughed and said you cannot fly without flapping feathers — until a supersonic fighter jet landed on their heads.»

[10:45 - 12:30] (165 words)
December 2013. Mark Zuckerberg flies to New York to lure NYU professor Yann LeCun into leading a new artificial intelligence lab — FAIR. LeCun issues an ultimatum that makes corporate lawyers' blood run cold: "I will join you on one condition only — we lock nothing away. Every paper, every framework, and every model must be published openly for all of humanity." Zuckerberg agrees. From this pact grows PyTorch — the open framework in which the overwhelming majority of the world's neural networks are written today — and the LLaMA family of open models, which broke the monopoly of Silicon Valley's closed corporations. When the heads of closed labs began frightening the U.S. Congress with the idea that open-source AI should be banned and licensed in order to "save humanity," LeCun stepped onto the stage and called things by their names: this is not concern for safety — it is an attempt by medieval guilds to ban Gutenberg's printing press, in order to keep their monopoly on selling books.

[12:30 - 14:45] (148 words)
But today, Yann LeCun throws his greatest heresy straight into the face of the entire generative AI industry. While the world goes mad over chatbots and predicts the arrival of AGI as soon as next year, simply by adding more chips to large language models, LeCun declares: "Large language models are an off-ramp on the highway to human-level intelligence." Why? Look at the math: to train a modern LLM, it is fed the entire public text of the internet. That is roughly ten to the thirteenth power of tokens of data. A human would need around a hundred and seventy thousand years of continuous reading just to skim through that text. And yet — a model that has read the entire library of humankind cannot learn to drive a car in twenty hours, the way any seventeen-year-old can, and does not have the intelligence of an ordinary house cat!

[14:45 - 16:30] (135 words)
In its first sixteen thousand waking hours, a four-year-old child receives through the optic nerves fifty times more information about the real three-dimensional world than is contained in all the texts of the internet combined! Language is only a flat, compressed shadow of reality. Most of our intelligence is nonverbal. When a carpenter planes a board, or a cat prepares to leap onto a cabinet, they do not generate sentences of words in their heads — a physical simulator of reality is running in their brain: a world model. This is what LeCun works on today, building the JEPA architecture — an artificial intelligence that does not guess the next word in a text, but learns to understand gravity, inertia, cause and effect, and the geometry of the living physical world in a space of high abstractions.

[16:30 - 18:00] (135 words)
When online debates reach the boiling point, sixty-four-year-old LeCun closes his laptop and heads out into the open ocean on his sailing yacht. A sailboat is the perfect physical metaphor for his philosophy. You cannot force the wind to blow where you want it to. But if you know the laws of aerodynamics and hydrodynamics flawlessly, you can set your sail at a sharp angle and sail close-hauled — straight into the oncoming wind. He looks at the future without any fear: in his conviction, we are not creating terminators that will want to enslave us, because the drive to dominate is a biological instinct of apes fighting over territory, not a property of intelligence. We are building a new Renaissance, in which every person on Earth will have their own staff of brilliant digital assistants.

[18:00 - 19:45] (157 words)
Which principles of this unbreakable French rationalist are worth adding to your own personal arsenal?

1. If the whole crowd is running in one direction — stop and look the other way. Fashion in science, business, and life is herd instinct. When a topic becomes mainstream, its main discoveries have already been made. Real breakthroughs always wait in the dark corners that the majority has declared "unpromising."

2. Never confuse eloquence with understanding. Just as a language model can smoothly generate plausible nonsense without understanding physics, people often hide emptiness behind fashionable words. Test ideas through contact with the hard physical world: does this scheme work in metal, in code, in real action?

3. Share knowledge openly — secrecy is a sign of weakness. Whoever is afraid to show their work to the world is afraid of being stopped. A strong creator opens their blueprints, because they know: while others copy yesterday's step, they are already three miles ahead.

[19:45 - 21:00] (43 words)
Fear of the unknown is born only from darkness in the mind. When you replace mystical awe with the cold light of engineering knowledge, the monsters disappear. And then the fiercest headwind of the era simply becomes a tailwind that fills your sails.
