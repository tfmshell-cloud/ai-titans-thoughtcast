[0:00 - 0:25] (83 words)
Every great scientific revolution has its generals, its merchants, and its diplomats. But at the very center of the whirlwind there always stands a person of a different breed — a monk of science, whose gaze reaches through the walls of corporate offices into a pure mathematical abyss. When Ilya Sutskever speaks in a half-dark room, it seems he is not choosing his words — he is trying to translate into human language something he has already seen in a space of a billion dimensions.

[0:25 - 1:45] (154 words)
His biography began at the fracture of an era. He was born in December 1986 in the closed Soviet city of Gorky, into the family of an engineer and inventor. When the boy turned five, the Soviet Union collapsed, and the family emigrated to Israel — to Jerusalem. The early experience of a child carried from an icy empire to the scorching sun of biblical hills shaped his first axiom: the outer world is fragile and changeable; the only unshakable foundation lies within one's own mind. School felt physically cramped to him. While his classmates studied basic fractions, fifteen-year-old Ilya enrolled on his own in the Open University of Israel. With no lectures and no supervisors, shut in his room with heavy volumes of linear algebra and the theory of algorithms, he learned the chief art of a thinker: to hold an unsolved problem in his head for weeks, without retreating before a blank wall.

[1:45 - 3:30] (135 words)
In 2002, the family uproots again and crosses the ocean — to Canada. Ilya is sixteen. He immediately becomes a student at the University of Toronto. And one day, the seventeen-year-old walks down the corridor of the computer science department, knocks on the door of office 4306, and asks a gray-haired man standing at a tall desk: "Do you have any work for me?" That man was Geoffrey Hinton. Hinton gave the young man three scientific papers on neural networks and asked him to come back in a week. Ilya came back in two days. He had not merely read the papers — he had found a mathematical inefficiency in them and written his own code that ran faster. Hinton understood: standing before him was a person with an intuition that comes along once in a generation.

[3:30 - 5:15] (145 words)
All his years in the Toronto lab, Sutskever was obsessed with one heretical idea that even experienced professors waved away. Everyone believed that intelligent behavior required highly complex, sophisticated algorithms. Sutskever saw something else: the backpropagation algorithm was already perfect. It lacked only one thing — scale. If you take a simple neural network and make it a thousand times larger, quantity will inevitably turn into a new, almost biological quality. In 2012, it was he who pushed for the creation of AlexNet, persuading Alex Krizhevsky to rewrite a convolutional network for NVIDIA graphics cards. And two years later, already at Google Brain, Sutskever carried out a second revolution: he created the sequence-to-sequence architecture, which for the first time allowed a neural network to absorb an entire sentence in English, compress it into a pure thought in vector space, and unfold it back out in French.

[5:15 - 6:45] (104 words)
In 2015, Sam Altman and Elon Musk invite twenty-nine-year-old Sutskever to become chief scientist and co-founder of OpenAI. For the sake of this mission, Ilya walks away from multimillion-dollar Google stock options and leaves the most powerful infrastructure in the world to start from scratch in an ordinary apartment in San Francisco. It is at OpenAI that Sutskever formulates the deepest philosophical law of our time — one that ninety-nine percent of artificial intelligence critics still do not understand. Skeptics say: "GPT language models are just statistical autocomplete that guesses the next word." To this, Sutskever replies with his famous analogy of the detective novel.

[6:45 - 8:45] (147 words)
Imagine you are reading a complex, three-hundred-page Agatha Christie detective novel. On the last page, the detective gathers everyone in the drawing room and says: "And so, the murderer in this room is..." And then comes a single word — the name of the killer. For a neural network to correctly predict that one next word, it is not enough to know the rules of grammar or how often words are used! To name the killer, the model must build within its weights a full simulation of the book's physical world: people's motives, the psychology of jealousy, the train timetable, the geometry of the rooms, and the laws of logic.

«Predicting the next word, at sufficient scale, is not linguistics. It is the reconstruction of the entire hidden reality of the human world from the shadows that reality casts into text. Perfect compression of information is intelligence itself.»

[8:45 - 11:15] (122 words)
When the paper on the Transformer architecture comes out in 2017, Sutskever instantly sees in it the reactor he has long been waiting for. He combines transformers with the hypothesis of limitless scaling and leads the creation of GPT-1, GPT-2, GPT-3, DALL-E, and GPT-4. Every time engineers said a model had hit the ceiling, Sutskever ordered the compute increased tenfold — and the ceiling disappeared. Before the researchers' eyes, deep within the matrices, emergent abilities suddenly woke up: a machine that had been trained only to continue text suddenly taught itself to write complex program code, solve olympiad physics problems, and explain subtle human irony. Sutskever understood: they were no longer writing programs. They were summoning a new kind of mind into being.

[11:15 - 13:00] (126 words)
As the models grew more powerful, the atmosphere around Ilya Sutskever changed. While the commercial division celebrated billion-dollar contracts and prepared press presentations, the chief scientist sank deeper and deeper into existential anxiety. At OpenAI's corporate retreats, he commissioned a wooden effigy from a sculptor, symbolizing an uncontrolled artificial intelligence, and set it on fire at night in front of the team, leading the engineers in a chant: "Feel the AGI! We will never release into the world a system that threatens human life!" In the summer of 2023, he steps away from building new products and leads the Superalignment group — a team that was supposed to find, within four years, a mathematical way to steer a mind thousands of times greater than a human one.

[13:00 - 15:00] (151 words)
And then comes the tragedy of November 2023. A chasm opens between the speed of Sam Altman's commercial empire and the apocalyptic caution of the scientist. Friday, November 17. It is Ilya Sutskever who reads Sam Altman the board's decision to fire him. He sincerely believed he was saving the world from an uncontrolled race. But a scientist who had spent his whole life among pure formulas and information theory had not accounted for human psychology, corporate alliances, and people's personal loyalty. When Greg Brockman's wife, in tears, asked Ilya why he was destroying the thing they had built together, and when seven hundred employees put their resignation letters on the table, the scientist's heart could not bear the split. He publishes one of the most dramatic lines in the history of technology: "I deeply regret my participation in the board's actions. I never intended to harm what we built together."

[15:00 - 16:45] (126 words)
May 2024. Ilya Sutskever closes the door of OpenAI behind him for good. He had learned the main lesson of that autumn: it is impossible to chase hundreds of millions of consumers in the chatbot market and, at the same time, preserve absolute purity in the safety of superintelligence. Commercial noise will always defeat caution. And in June 2024, in Palo Alto and Tel Aviv, he founds an organization unlike any company in the world — Safe Superintelligence, SSI:

«We have one goal, one focus, and one product: a safe superintelligence. We will not release intermediate products, we have no customers, and we are fully insulated from the pressure of quarterly reports. We will come out of the silence only when safety and intelligence have become one.»

[16:45 - 18:15] (111 words)
Investors gave him a billion dollars on the strength of his name alone and a promise to sell nothing until the final breakthrough. Today, Ilya Sutskever is once again where he was strongest — at fifteen in Jerusalem, and at twenty in a Toronto basement: in a small room with a handful of the planet's best mathematicians, with no press releases, no marketing, alone with the fundamental equation of being. He is searching for a new formula — the next step after the age of pretraining, when artificial minds begin to think not with the breadth of the internet they have read, but with the depth of their own inner search for truth.

[18:15 - 20:15] (167 words)
What laws of character does the path of Ilya Sutskever reveal to us?

1. Compress the noise down to first causes. True intelligence is the ability to discard thousands of surface details and see the one underlying law that governs the whole system. Do not scatter your mind across a hundred fashionable methods — find one fundamental lever and scale it to the absolute.

2. Protect your inner silence from the bustle of the market. The greatest danger to a great goal is short-term success and the noise of a crowd demanding an instant show. If you want to create something lasting, you must have the courage to close the door and work for years in silence, without applause.

3. Do not be afraid to ask naive, childlike questions. Adult experts often go blind because they are afraid of looking incompetent. The entire deep learning revolution grew out of Ilya's childlike question: "What if we just make the network a hundred times bigger?" The truth is always simple.

[20:15 - 21:45] (57 words)
The world around us shouts, hurries, and bargains for every second of attention. But the true tectonic shifts of history happen without a sound. They are born at that point of absolute stillness where a person stops listening to the din of the marketplace — and begins to hear the quiet music of the laws of the universe.
